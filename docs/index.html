Living PRD (Product Requirements Document)

Updated every week as we test with real users and learn what works.

1. Target User & The Job to Be Done

Who is this for? Comms firms that do news roundups for clients across multiple domains. E.g., when I worked at a PR agency, we would do a weekly newsletter for a B2B payments company, focused on news across FX, macroeconomics, fraud, etc..

The painful "Before" workflow: We manually search the web for the most relevant news in those categories from that week. We have access to subscriptions, but the company does not so if there's a paywall, they won't be able to read it.

The "After" workflow with this product: The tool scours the web for the relevant articles that week per category, explains why each one made the cut, and gives a 2–3 sentence summary written from the article itself. It hyperlinks the direct link to the site. It learns what each client likes from newsletters the agency already sent them. For articles that require a subscription, users can upload a PDF of the article and the tool will include it in the newsletter and summarize it for them. The tool summarizes and links them without having to store login credentials.

2. Scope & Explicit Non-Goals

  • In Scope (Core Workflow): Automates newsletter creation based on industry categories and solves the paywall issue. It finds candidate articles per category from news feeds and search, then ranks them by relevance. It drafts a 3-sentence summary of each article. User reviews and edits the picks then is able to email the newsletter directly to client.
  • Out of Scope #1: Sending newsletters to clients automatically. The tool only produces a draft newsletter that a human approves and sends through the agency's email.
  • Out of Scope #2: Getting around paywalls in any way: no archive sites, no reading behind logins, no storing an agency's subscription passwords. The tool uses news search APIs, RSS feeds and publicly available pages; paywalled articles come in only as a PDF the user uploads.
  • Out of Scope #3: Storing or redistributing full articles or uploaded files; PDFs, Word files and article text are used only to write a summary or a client profile, then discarded.
  • Out of Scope #4: Press releases and companies' own announcements, and articles not in English. (A news outlet's report on a competitor's product launch is in scope.)

3. How It Works Under the Hood & Riskiest Assumption (PRD v3 · Build 4 week)

Riskiest Assumption: AI can pick the same articles a PR professional would pick for a client's category, and can summarize them accurately from only the headline and a short description, without reading paywalled full text. If either part fails, the user has to redo the search and rewrite every summary manually.

How Build 2 tests it: One full user action now runs on data with an AI call. For each category, Claude (Haiku 5.5) scores 20 candidate articles from 0 to 10 and summarizes the top 5.

  • Picking: Before AI scoring, ranking was "priority source first, then newest." With Claude scoring, the same search returned fake-bank-account scams, card fraud results and a central bank fraud-reporting rule. Scoring is based on criteria including topic fit, client impact, newsworthiness, and source & format.
  • Summarizing: The first summaries were filler ("It is a daily currency market update"). I tightened the instructions, although the summaries are still pretty thin because it only has access to the headline and subheadline. Need to figure out a way to get more of the article body.
  • Freshness: Google News occasionally returned old stories (one from 2015), so every source now has a hard 7-day cutoff.
  • Still unproven: whether a PR professional agrees with Claude's picks. Every removal is saved with Claude's score, which sets up the Build 4 evaluation.

What changed after the first real test run (Build 3): I removed 13 of the 15 articles that made the cut. The fixes, one phase at a time: duplicate stories merged; every score explained, with a stricter 7/10 cutoff that learns from my past removals; summaries written from the full article text; English only and no press releases; and a client profile learned from past newsletters. The Build 4 evaluation reruns the same three categories to measure the difference.

How the app works:

  1. The user signs in, then enters the client, companies to watch, priority sources and categories (with optional search terms, excluded words, and a "What it means for this client" description that keeps an ambiguous name like "Hardware Security" on topic). They can upload past newsletters (PDF or Word .docx) for the client: Claude writes an editable "What this client likes" profile (topics, companies, outlets, kinds of stories) and suggests outlets to add as priority sources. Only the profile is saved, never the files.
  2. For each category, the server searches Exa twice (once limited to the client's priority outlets, once open with 20 results; news from the last 7 days, with excerpts from each article), the NYT Article Search API, and 10 publisher RSS feeds as backup. Press-release services (PR Newswire, Business Wire, GlobeNewswire…) are blocked.
  3. Articles older than 7 days, without a date, or mostly in another alphabet are dropped, and exact repeats (same link or headline) are removed.
  4. The best 30 candidates go to Claude in one request, with the client profile and the user's own recent removals and reasons. For each article Claude returns the rubric scores below, a one-sentence reason, a short summary, whether it is in English, and which other articles cover the same story.
  5. Non-English articles are dropped. Duplicate stories are merged: one article per story is kept (a priority outlet first, then the fullest text, then the earliest), and the others are listed as "Also covered by".
  6. Only articles scoring at least 7/10 and mainly about the category (topic fit at least 3 of 4) make the newsletter, up to 5 per category; the rest go to a "filtered" pile with their reason, where the user can still include one. A category can end up with fewer than 5, or none.
  7. For the articles that made the cut, the server fetches the article text (Exa contents, free outlets only) and Claude rewrites each summary in 2–3 sentences: what happened, the key detail, and why it matters. Paywalled outlets keep the summary from the public excerpt (see the paywall rule below).
  8. The user reorders or removes articles (with a reason) and adds stories by hand: paste a link and Fill from link reads it (free outlets only), or upload a PDF of a paywalled article. Then the user copies an email-ready draft, which is saved to Past editions.
  9. If Claude or the full-text step is unavailable, the app falls back (ranking by source and date, or excerpt summaries), so the workflow never breaks.

Scoring rubric (out of 10; each card shows the parts and "Why it's here"):

PartPointsFull marks when
Topic fit0–4The article is mainly about this category, as defined by "What it means for this client". Another meaning of the name scores 0.
Client impact0–2It names the client or a company to watch, or directly affects their business, customers, regulators or competitors.
Newsworthiness0–2A specific new development from the last few days, not a recap, preview, follow-up with little new, or evergreen piece.
Source quality0–2A straight news report from one of the client's priority outlets or another major outlet.

Press releases and companies' own announcements, opinion pieces, podcasts, advice columns, sponsored and lifestyle content are capped at 2/10 (a news outlet's report on a launch is not capped). Cutoff: at least 7/10 and topic fit at least 3/4. If the client has a profile from past newsletters, topics, companies and outlets in it raise topic fit and client impact, but an article still has to be mainly about the category.

Paywall rule: Outlets on the paywalled list (WSJ, NYT, FT, Bloomberg, Barron's, The Economist, The Information, The Washington Post; editable in app/outlets.json) are never fetched. Their summary comes only from the public excerpt or abstract the search returns. To summarize one properly, the user uploads a PDF of an article they're allowed to read. Press Pass never uses archive sites, never reads behind a login and never stores publisher passwords. Free outlets get a summary written from the full article text.

Simple Architecture: Client setup (categories, keywords, descriptions, priority sources, profile learned from past newsletters) → server pulls this week's articles from Exa, NYT and publisher RSS feeds → 7-day cutoff, English only, no press releases, exact de-duplication → Claude Haiku 5.5 scores with the rubric, explains each score and marks same-story duplicates, using the client profile and the user's past removals → duplicates merged, 7/10 cutoff → full-text summaries for the finalists (free outlets only) → user reviews and edits → user copies the newsletter draft and sends it to the client. Build 3 · Build.exe: The Core Workflow (Due Nov 23)

  • What shipped: Press Pass is live at ss11807-battlecardbuilder.onrender.com (Beginner lane: Ship it). People sign in with email and password (Supabase Auth), and each account keeps its own saved client setups, past newsletters and article-removal feedback, with Row Level Security on every table. Search moved to Exa (news from the last 7 days, with article highlights) plus the NYT Article Search API, and each category has a new "What it means for this client" field to keep results on topic. The paid routes need a sign-in and have per-user hourly limits. Full audit in SECURITY.md.
  • What broke / changed in PRD: My first real test run failed: I removed 13 of the 15 articles that made the cut. "Hardware Security" pulled in BitLocker and Nvidia stories instead of crypto hardware wallets, the same Coinbase story appeared twice, summaries just repeated the headline (Google News only provides headlines), and every card said 9/10 with no reason. Switching to Exa and NYT and adding category descriptions is the first fix; duplicate detection, explained scores with a stricter cutoff, and real summaries come next. The first Render deploy failed because the service was connected to an old copy of the class template instead of this repo. The live security check found that the server's own code was downloadable (no secrets in it), so the site now serves only its three public files. Email confirmation is off because Supabase's free email can't reach outside addresses.
  • Things learned with AI: How Row Level Security keeps each user's rows private, why a publishable key can sit in the browser but a secret key can't, what changes when an app leaves localhost (PORT, environment variables, files that vanish on restart), rate limits as a spending guard, and pinning a CDN library with an integrity hash.
  • Security test: Signed in as a second account on the live site: it sees "No newsletters yet" and none of the first account's data. A scripted check on the live site passed 39/39 (no reading, changing, deleting or planting another account's rows), and the paid routes refused requests without a sign-in.
  • Cost & time this week: $0.09 ($0.04 Claude API + $0.05 Exa search; NYT, Render and Supabase are free) · 5 hours

Build 4 week · Fixing what the first real test run found (Nov 23–26)

  • What shipped: Six phases, each tested by me before it went live. (1) Duplicate stories are merged into one, with "Also covered by" links. (2) Every article shows "Why it's here" and its rubric score, and only articles with at least 7/10 and a strong topic fit make the newsletter; Claude also learns from my past removals and their reasons. (3) Summaries for the articles that make the cut are written from the full article text (free outlets only), and manually added links get a summary too ("Fill from link"). (4) English-only results, and no press releases or companies' own announcements; news reports of a competitor's launch still count. (5) Upload past newsletters for a client (PDF or .docx) and Claude writes an editable "What this client likes" profile that feeds the scoring and suggests outlets. (6) A newspaper-style redesign with a Press Pass masthead on every page, a Past editions sidebar, a one-line "Add a story" bar and quieter AI details.
  • What broke / changed in PRD: A Coinbase CEO story I expected in Cold Wallet was missing. I first suspected my earlier removal of a low-quality copy of it, but the real cause was the search: Exa only returned 10 results and the story ran on small outlets. The open search now returns 20 and the companies to watch steer it. Removal reasons are now applied for what they mean ("Low-quality source" marks down the outlet, not the story). I dropped the planned "Read on [Outlet] →" card for paywalled articles: they simply keep the excerpt summary. My first newspaper layout (lead story plus columns) was hard to edit, so the review page went back to one story per row with the new styling. Old .doc files can't be read on the free server, so users re-save them as .docx.
  • Things learned with AI: Debugging a missing result by checking each step (search, scoring, cutoff) instead of guessing, why a database change needs a backup first, how to check a design with a mockup before rebuilding, and that "paywalled" can be a simple editable list instead of clever detection.
